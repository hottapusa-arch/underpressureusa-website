Aging Infrastructure & Replacement

Why age alone does not tell the whole story.

← All Infrastructure Failures topics

Old does not automatically mean failed

A water or sewer network often contains pipe installed over many decades. Some older sections remain reliable, while newer ones may have trouble because of installation damage, aggressive soils, operating conditions, or poor support. Installation year is useful information, but it is not a complete condition assessment.

Condition and consequence are different questions

Condition asks how likely a component is to fail. Consequence asks what happens if it does. A small main with repeated minor leaks may be troublesome; a critical transmission main serving a hospital may deserve attention even without a recent break. Utilities need to consider both when prioritizing limited funding.

What belongs in the decision

Material, diameter, break history, corrosion environment, soil conditions, pressure behavior, inspection findings, repair frequency, and service importance all contribute. For sewers, capacity, infiltration, structural defects, and overflow risk matter as well. Missing or unreliable records should be recognized rather than treated as certainty.

Replacement is not the only tool

Depending on the problem, maintenance, targeted repairs, rehabilitation, improved monitoring, or operational changes may extend useful service life. Other situations call for full replacement, especially where failures are frequent, access is difficult, or the consequence of an outage is high. The right answer depends on the system and its risks.

Why field history matters

A crew that repeatedly returns to the same stretch knows something the age column in an asset spreadsheet does not show. Recording failure types, repair locations, and valve or access problems can improve capital planning. The goal is to spend money before the most disruptive failures, not simply replace the oldest pipe first.

The takeaway

A good replacement program is based on risk, condition, and service importance—not birthdays.

Educational overview only. Field work requires utility-approved procedures, qualified personnel, and applicable safety requirements.
