Air Release & Vacuum Valves

Why air management is part of protecting a pressurized pipeline.

← Explore all valves & fittings

Why air needs a path

Air can enter a pipeline during filling, maintenance, or certain operating conditions. Dissolved gases can also come out of solution. Because air behaves differently from water, pockets of trapped gas may reduce capacity, cause unstable flow, or complicate pressure readings. Designers provide ways for air to leave and, in some circumstances, enter a pipeline safely.

Three functions people often confuse

An air-release valve is intended to vent relatively small amounts of accumulated air while the pipeline is pressurized. An air/vacuum valve handles larger volumes of air during filling and draining. Combination air valves bring those functions together in one assembly. The names describe different duties, and not every valve is suitable for every location.

Why vacuum protection matters

When a pipeline drains or pressure falls, a vacuum can develop if air cannot enter quickly enough. Depending on the pipe material, wall strength, installation, and conditions, negative pressure can threaten the pipe or draw unwanted material inward through a defect. A properly designed air/vacuum arrangement helps manage those conditions.

Where they are commonly considered

High points, long rising sections, and certain pump or transmission-main configurations may need air management. Actual placement is based on hydraulic analysis, operating conditions, and the system’s geometry. Installing an air valve wherever a pipe rises is not a substitute for design.

Why they need attention

Air valves can become fouled, leak, or fail to open and close as intended. The discharge arrangement and location also matter for safety and sanitary protection. A device intended to protect the system can become a problem if it is inaccessible, poorly maintained, or installed in an unsuitable environment.
