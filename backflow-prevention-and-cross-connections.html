Backflow Prevention & Cross-Connections

How unwanted flow can carry contaminants into drinking-water piping—and how utilities prevent it.

← All Water Systems topics

What is a cross-connection?

A cross-connection is an actual or potential connection between a potable water supply and a source of contamination. A hose submerged in a chemical tank, a boiler connected without proper protection, or an irrigation system with chemical injection can create a pathway that should not exist without suitable safeguards.

A cross-connection does not automatically mean contaminated water is flowing into the main. The concern is what could happen if pressure conditions change or another source pushes water in the wrong direction.

Two ways backflow happens

Backsiphonage occurs when supply pressure drops enough to draw water backward through an unprotected connection. A main break, heavy firefighting demand, or certain system operations can contribute to a pressure drop.

Backpressure happens when pressure on the customer or equipment side exceeds the supply pressure. Pumps, elevated equipment, or heated systems can create this condition. The two mechanisms are different, but either can move nonpotable water toward a drinking-water supply.

The simplest separation: an air gap

A properly designed air gap is an unobstructed vertical separation between the water outlet and the flood-level rim of a receiving vessel. Because there is no direct pipe connection across that space, an air gap can provide strong protection when it is correctly sized and maintained.

A hose hanging over a tank rim is not necessarily an adequate air gap. If the hose can slip into the liquid, the protection can disappear.

Common prevention devices and assemblies

Protection is selected for the actual hazard and pressure conditions—not just by pipe diameter. Examples include reduced-pressure-principle assemblies (RP), double-check-valve assemblies (DC), pressure vacuum breakers (PVB), and atmospheric vacuum breakers (AVB). Their permitted uses are not interchangeable.

A reduced-pressure assembly provides a relief zone intended to discharge under certain failure conditions and needs appropriate drainage and clearance. A double-check assembly may be suitable for some lower-hazard applications but is not a universal answer for high-hazard connections. Vacuum breakers primarily address backsiphonage and have installation and use limitations.

The water supplier and applicable plumbing and cross-connection-control requirements determine what protection is acceptable. Some connections require a physical air gap instead of a mechanical assembly.

Where crews encounter the risk

Temporary water connections, hydrant meters, filling operations, irrigation systems, chemical-feed equipment, industrial processes, and building mechanical systems can all create cross-connection questions. A connection that looks routine in the field may still require specific protection.

When setting up a temporary water supply, crews should verify the approved connection point, intended use, required backflow protection, and who is responsible for testing or monitoring. Never assume a hose connection is safe simply because the water is flowing outward at the moment.

Inspection, testing, and maintenance

Mechanical assemblies contain parts that can foul, wear, freeze, or fail. Many jurisdictions require periodic testing of testable assemblies by qualified personnel, with records maintained by the owner or water supplier. Requirements and intervals vary.

A successful installation is not the end of cross-connection control. Utilities also need hazard surveys, changes-of-use reviews, enforcement procedures, and a process for addressing failed tests or unprotected connections.

The field takeaway

Backflow prevention is about protecting the drinking-water supply when normal flow and pressure cannot be taken for granted. Identify the potential contamination source, understand the pressure conditions, and use the protection required for that particular connection.
