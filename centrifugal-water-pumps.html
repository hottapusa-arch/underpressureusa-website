Centrifugal Water Pumps

Centrifugal pumps are widely used to move water through treatment plants and distribution systems.

← All Pumps & Pressure topics

What a centrifugal pump actually does

Inside a centrifugal pump, a motor turns an impeller. Water enters near the center and is directed outward through the casing. The rotating impeller adds energy to the water; the piping system determines how much flow the pump can actually deliver. The pump does not create water, and it cannot overcome every restriction just by running harder.

The parts worth knowing

The impeller moves water, the casing collects and directs it, and the shaft connects the impeller to the driver. Bearings support the rotating assembly. A mechanical seal or other shaft-sealing arrangement limits leakage where the shaft passes through the casing. On the suction side, piping and available inlet pressure matter just as much as the pump itself.

Head is not the same as pressure

Pump head describes energy added per unit weight of water, commonly expressed in feet. Pressure is what a gauge reads at a particular point. A pump adding 100 feet of head corresponds to roughly 43 psi of pressure increase for water under suitable conditions, but the actual gauge reading also depends on elevation, existing inlet pressure, and losses. That is why two gauges on the same pump can tell different parts of the story.

Why the flow changes

A pump has a performance curve showing how much head it can produce at different flow rates. The pipe network has its own resistance: long runs, fittings, valves, and elevation all affect the demand on the pump. Where the pump curve and system curve meet is the operating point. Closing a valve can reduce flow without changing the pump itself.

What crews may notice

Unusual noise, vibration, loss of capacity, high temperature, or repeated seal problems are clues worth investigating. Air entering the suction, inadequate suction conditions, or operation outside the intended range can all contribute. Cavitation is one possible problem when local pressure falls low enough for vapor bubbles to form and collapse. It should not be diagnosed from noise alone.

Where to learn more

Pumps are one part of a larger water system. Storage tanks, pressure zones, control valves, and customer demand all influence their operation.
