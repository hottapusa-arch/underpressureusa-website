Corrosion & Pipe Deterioration

What happens when pipe walls slowly lose strength.

← All Infrastructure Failures topics

What is happening?

Corrosion is a chemical or electrochemical process that can damage metallic pipe over time. It does not look the same everywhere.

Why it happens

External corrosion depends on soil, moisture, electrical conditions, and protection systems. Internal corrosion depends on water chemistry, flow, deposits, and pipe material. Some failures involve localized pits rather than uniform wall loss.

What crews look for

An exposed pipe may show rust, tuberculation, pitting, or thinning. Appearance alone does not establish remaining strength; utilities may need condition assessment and engineering review.

What we can learn

Coatings, linings, cathodic protection where appropriate, water-quality management, and replacement planning can reduce risk.

What matters in the field

Corrosion can attack pipe from the outside, inside, or both. Soil and water chemistry, stray currents, coatings, and dissimilar metals all influence what happens.

A practical example

A rusty exterior does not by itself tell you how much sound wall remains, and a clean-looking pipe may still have hidden deterioration.

Not all corrosion looks alike

External corrosion may be driven by soil moisture, chemistry, and electrical conditions, while internal corrosion depends heavily on water chemistry and flow. A pipe can lose wall thickness unevenly, leaving pits that are much more serious than the average condition suggests. Coatings, linings, and cathodic protection are tools, not universal cures.
