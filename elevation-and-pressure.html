Elevation and Water Pressure

Elevation changes help explain why pressure differs across a distribution system.

← All Pumps & Pressure topics

Why elevation changes the gauge reading

Water has weight. In a still column of water, each vertical foot represents about 0.433 pounds per square inch of pressure. That means a 100-foot difference in elevation corresponds to about 43.3 psi of hydrostatic pressure difference for water. It is a useful relationship, but it does not mean every home 100 feet downhill will always read exactly 43 psi more.

The water surface is the reference

Imagine an elevated tank connected to a main. With no flow and ignoring other effects, the vertical distance between the water surface and a gauge helps determine static pressure at that gauge. As the tank level rises or falls, the available static pressure changes. A pump or pressure-control device can change the picture.

Static pressure versus flowing pressure

Static pressure is measured when water is not moving at the point of interest. When water flows, energy is lost to friction and fittings, and pressure readings can change. A gauge that looks acceptable at night may read lower during heavy demand or hydrant use. That is why one reading should never be treated as a complete description of the system.

What pressure zones do

Cities with hills often divide distribution networks into pressure zones. Pumps, tanks, and pressure-regulating valves help keep service pressures within the intended range. Without that planning, a high neighborhood might struggle for pressure while a lower area experiences excessive pressure.

A field example

Suppose two gauges connect to the same static water column and one is 50 feet lower. The lower gauge would be expected to read about 21.7 psi higher, assuming the same reference water level and no complicating equipment. During flow, the difference may not match that simple calculation because friction and operating conditions also matter.

Where this leads

Elevation is one of the simplest ways to begin understanding a water network, but it is only one piece. Water Pressure Fundamentals connects elevation with flow, pumps, pressure zones, and equipment ratings in practical language.
