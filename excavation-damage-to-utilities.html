Excavation Damage to Utilities

One bucket can interrupt service to a neighborhood.

← All Infrastructure Failures topics

What is happening?

Buried water and sewer infrastructure often shares crowded corridors with gas, electric, and communications utilities.

Why it happens

Damage can occur when a facility is unmarked, marks are misunderstood, records are incomplete, or excavation proceeds without adequate verification.

What crews look for

A struck main may leak immediately or suffer damage that becomes apparent later. Crews must account for adjacent utilities and site safety before repair.

What we can learn

Notification, utility locating, careful exposure, and coordination help reduce risk. Requirements depend on local law and site conditions.

What matters in the field

Utility strikes can damage more than the line being exposed. Pressure, electrical energy, gas, traffic, and surrounding structures may create additional hazards.

A practical example

A small nick in a pipe or cable can become a later failure; apparent lack of immediate damage is not proof of safety.

A marked line is not an exposed line

Utility records and surface marks help identify risk, but they do not prove exact depth or alignment. Service lines, abandoned facilities, and undocumented repairs can complicate the picture. Verification methods and careful excavation near known facilities are central to damage prevention.

Not all strikes come from an excavator

Backhoes, trenchers, graders, augers, post-hole diggers, saws, pile drivers, and hand tools can all damage buried infrastructure. So can trenchless construction. Horizontal directional drilling (HDD), guided boring, pipe bursting, and other underground installation methods may cross existing lines without a visible open trench. The absence of an excavator bucket does not mean the existing utilities are safe.

Directional drilling and unseen crossings

A directional-drill pilot bore or reamer can strike or scrape a water main, sewer force main, gravity sewer, gas line, electric duct bank, or communications conduit. Drilling fluid may also enter a cracked or open pipe, and a crossing can damage a pipe or its support without causing an immediate surface leak. Sewer lines are especially vulnerable to an unnoticed bore through the pipe: the obstruction or intrusion may be discovered later through a blockage, backup, CCTV inspection, or failure. A drill path needs verified clearance, not just confidence in a drawing.

Damage without an immediate break

A pipe can be nicked, gouged, scored, dented, pulled out of alignment, or left with damaged coating or bedding. Cast iron and brittle older materials may fracture; plastic may develop a crack or stress concentration; steel can lose protective coating and corrode later. A sewer joint may separate without any pressurized leak to announce it. Damage to a pressure line can show up later when loads or pressure change.

What prevention looks like

Use the applicable one-call notification process, review utility records, coordinate with facility owners, and verify locations and depths where the work could conflict. Potholing or other suitable exposure methods can establish actual clearance at critical crossings. Directional-drilling plans need attention to entry and exit points, bore path, depth, reaming envelope, and existing utilities. Follow local laws and utility requirements; paint marks and maps are starting points, not guarantees.

The wider consequence

A strike may interrupt drinking water, spill sewage, damage a reclaimed-water line, release gas, interrupt power, or affect industrial process piping. Crews must consider the fluid or energy inside the facility, the people nearby, and the possibility of contamination or service loss. If a line is contacted, follow the facility owner’s reporting and safety procedures even when the damage seems minor.
