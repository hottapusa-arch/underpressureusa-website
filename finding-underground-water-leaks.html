Finding Underground Water Leaks

The leak is not always where water reaches the surface.

← All Infrastructure Failures topics

Why the wet spot can fool you

Water escaping from a buried main does not always rise directly above the break. It can follow gravel bedding, old trenches, service-line paths, or a nearby utility corridor before appearing at the surface. A wet patch identifies an area to investigate, not necessarily the place to excavate.

Start with the system evidence

Look for changes in district flow, unexpected night demand, declining pressure, meter discrepancies, and customer reports. A sudden increase can point toward a new failure; a slow trend may suggest a smaller leak or another operational change. Compare observations against normal system patterns before drawing conclusions.

How crews narrow the search

Listening devices can pick up leak noise at hydrants, valves, or accessible fittings. Correlators compare sound arrival times at two locations; their results depend on accurate pipe length, material, and sound velocity assumptions. Pressure logging and step testing may help isolate a suspect zone where utility procedures allow. Plastic pipe, deep burial, traffic, and other background noise can complicate acoustic work.

Confirm before digging

Check maps and utility records, verify pipe size and material where possible, and locate other buried services. Correlation results and surface clues should be checked against one another. Excavation must follow utility-locating, shoring, traffic, and other site safety requirements.

What the repair should teach us

Record the actual failure point, pipe condition, joint type, soil conditions, and repair method. Those observations can improve future leak detection and help determine whether the event was isolated or part of a larger deterioration pattern.
