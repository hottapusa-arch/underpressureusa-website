Infiltration, Inflow & Overflows

How extra water can overwhelm a sewer system.

← All Infrastructure Failures topics

What is happening?

Sanitary sewers are designed to collect wastewater. Rain and groundwater entering the system can consume capacity that would otherwise carry wastewater.

Why it happens

Infiltration is groundwater entering through defects. Inflow is water entering more directly, such as through improper connections or openings. Heavy rainfall can expose both problems.

What crews look for

Extra flow can contribute to backups and sanitary sewer overflows, especially when combined with blockages or limited downstream capacity.

What we can learn

Smoke testing, flow monitoring, CCTV, repairs, and source removal are among the tools utilities use to reduce unwanted water.

What matters in the field

Infiltration is groundwater entering through defects; inflow is stormwater entering through direct pathways. Both can overload sewer collection and treatment capacity.

A practical example

A pump station that struggles only during storms may be receiving far more water than normal sanitary flow.

Two different paths into a sewer

Infiltration is groundwater entering through defects such as cracks and joints. Inflow is stormwater entering more directly through sources such as improper connections or covers. Both consume collection and treatment capacity, especially during wet weather, but they call for different investigations.
