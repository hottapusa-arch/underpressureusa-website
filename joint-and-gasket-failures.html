Joint & Gasket Failures

The pipe can be sound while the connection leaks.

← All Infrastructure Failures topics

The connection can fail before the pipe does

A pipe barrel may look sound while water escapes at a bell, coupling, flange, or mechanical joint. Each connection is designed to accommodate particular pipe dimensions, pressure, movement, and assembly requirements. A joint that works in one application is not automatically suitable for another.

How joints begin to leak

A gasket can be rolled, pinched, cut, contaminated, or seated incorrectly. Pipe ends can be misaligned or inserted to the wrong depth. Settlement can pull a joint apart, while pressure thrust can move an unrestrained fitting. Corrosion or damaged sealing surfaces can also prevent a tight seal. More than one of these conditions may be present.

What the field evidence tells you

First distinguish a leaking joint from a crack in the nearby pipe wall. Water can travel along the outside of a main before reaching the surface, so the wet spot is not always directly above the defect. Exposed joint alignment, pipe insertion marks, visible movement, and the condition of bolts or glands can help narrow the cause. Never assume the first visible leak is the only problem.

Why restraint matters

At bends, tees, dead ends, and other changes in direction, internal pressure creates forces that must be resisted. Depending on the design, restraint may come from restrained joints, thrust blocks, or other engineered measures. A new gasket will not solve a connection that is repeatedly being pulled apart by movement or thrust.

Repair the cause, not just the symptom

The repair decision depends on the joint type, pipe condition, accessibility, operating pressure, and utility requirements. A localized coupling repair may be appropriate in one case; another may need realignment, improved support, or a properly designed restrained assembly. Before returning a repaired main to service, follow the utility’s inspection, testing, disinfection, and flushing requirements as applicable.

The takeaway

A leaking connection is a clue. Ask why the seal was lost, whether the pipe moved, and whether the surrounding installation will support the repair over time.

Educational overview only. Field work requires utility-approved procedures, qualified personnel, and applicable safety requirements.
