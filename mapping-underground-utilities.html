The History of Underground Utility Mapping

From paper records to GIS and digital asset inventories.

← All History & Evolution topics

The paper era

Utilities recorded pipe routes, sizes, and valves on drawings, field books, and paper maps. Accuracy depended on updates and survey practices.

Why records drift

Repairs, abandoned lines, undocumented changes, and old reference points can leave records incomplete.

Digital mapping

GIS allows utilities to organize locations and attributes, but a digital map is only as reliable as its underlying data.

Finding buried infrastructure

Field locating, records review, potholing, and other verification methods remain necessary. A map is not a substitute for safe locating practices.

What matters in the field

Maps, GIS, surface features, and locating equipment each provide clues, but records may be incomplete or inaccurate.

A practical example

A line drawn on a screen is a starting point for verification, not proof of exact depth or position.

Maps are records, not guarantees

GIS and as-built drawings help locate assets and understand network connectivity, but old records may omit abandoned mains, offsets, and undocumented repairs. Crews combine records with surface features, locating methods, and physical verification before critical work.
