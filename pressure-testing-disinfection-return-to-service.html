Pressure Testing, Disinfection & Return to Service

Why a pressure-tight water main still needs cleaning, disinfection, and clearance before service.

← All Field Operations topics

Three different questions

A new or repaired potable-water main must satisfy different checks. Pressure testing evaluates leakage and the ability of the installed pipe and appurtenances to withstand the specified test conditions. Flushing removes debris and helps exchange construction water. Disinfection and required bacteriological sampling address the sanitary condition of the main. Passing one step does not automatically satisfy the others.

Before the pressure test

Confirm that restraints, thrust blocks where used, fittings, end caps, and test boundaries are ready for the planned pressure. Some concrete thrust blocks need curing time before pressurization. Isolate equipment not rated for the test and follow the approved procedure for filling, venting air, test pressure, duration, and acceptable leakage. Test values vary with pipe material, project specifications, and governing standards; a single pressure or time is not correct for every job.

Watch the complete assembly

Test boundaries may include valves, sleeves, fittings, joints, and temporary connections. A sound pipe with a leaking fitting still fails the purpose of the test. For tapping work, test the tapping sleeve and tapping valve as one assembly when required by the approved installation procedure: the pipe-to-sleeve seal, sleeve-to-valve connection, and valve body or bonnet must all be considered.

Flushing and disinfecting

Follow the utility-approved method and applicable requirements, commonly based on AWWA C651 for potable-water mains. Cleaning, flushing velocity, disinfectant concentration, contact time, and dechlorination are project- and method-specific. Chlorinated discharge can harm waterways and must be managed according to permits and local rules. Protect the main from contamination during filling and sampling.

Returning a main to service

After the required pressure test, disinfection, flushing, and bacteriological results are accepted, operators can follow the approved return-to-service sequence. Confirm valve positions, restore flow in a controlled manner, manage air, and check for leaks or unexpected pressure changes. Emergency repairs may use different authorized procedures; never substitute an informal field shortcut for utility or regulatory requirements.

The field takeaway

Pressure-tight does not mean sanitary, and clear-looking water does not prove it is safe. Document what was tested, the results, who accepted them, and the steps taken before the line was put back in service.
