Infiltration and Inflow

Why unwanted water enters sanitary sewers and overwhelms capacity.

← All Sewer Systems topics

Infiltration and inflow are different

Infiltration is groundwater entering sanitary sewers through cracks, joints, service connections, or manhole defects. Inflow is rainwater entering more directly through openings, covers, yard drains, roof leaders, or other connections. Both add water that does not belong in a separate sanitary sewer.

Why wet weather exposes the problem

A rapid spike during a storm can suggest direct inflow. Flow that stays high after rainfall can point toward groundwater infiltration, although real systems often have both. The pattern is a clue, not a diagnosis by itself.

What extra water costs

Unwanted water uses pipe capacity, increases pump run time, and sends additional flow to treatment. In severe conditions it can contribute to backups or overflows. The impact depends on the system, its available capacity, and the storm.

How crews narrow the search

Utilities may combine rainfall and flow records with CCTV, manhole inspections, smoke testing, dye testing, and targeted field checks. Each method answers a different question; camera footage alone will not identify every connection or hidden leak.

Match the repair to the pathway

Sealing a leaking joint may reduce groundwater infiltration, but it will not correct a roof drain tied into the sanitary sewer. Likewise, disconnecting an improper stormwater connection will not fix a cracked main below the water table. Find the source before choosing the fix.

What matters in the field

Record when and where abnormal flow appears, whether the ground is saturated, and whether defects occur at mains, manholes, or laterals. Those observations help distinguish a systemwide capacity issue from a localized source of unwanted water.

Educational overview only. Utility policies, site conditions, safety procedures, and applicable regulations govern field work.
