Why Sewer Pipes Fail

Gravity sewers and force mains fail in different ways.

← All Infrastructure Failures topics

Not every sewer failure looks like a collapse

A gravity sewer can keep carrying wastewater while cracks, separated joints, roots, or groundwater intrusion steadily make its condition worse. A force main is different: it carries wastewater under pressure and can develop leaks or failures related to pressure, corrosion, or air management.

Gravity sewer trouble

Gravity systems depend on a continuous downhill path. Settlement can create a low spot where solids collect. Roots may enter through defects and restrict flow. Joint separation can admit groundwater and soil, sometimes undermining the surrounding ground. Cracks and deformation may reduce structural capacity long before a complete collapse occurs.

Force mains face different risks

A force main receives flow from pumps and must withstand operating pressure and changes in pressure when equipment starts, stops, or valves operate. Material deterioration, corrosion, poor support, and pressure transients can contribute to failure. Because the main is pressurized, its inspection and isolation needs differ from those of a gravity sewer.

Read the symptoms carefully

Recurring blockages do not automatically mean the pipe needs more cleaning. The same trouble spot may indicate a sag, displaced joint, root entry, or damaged lining. CCTV can reveal many gravity sewer defects, while pump records, pressure behavior, leak history, and specialized testing may help assess force mains. No single inspection method answers every question.

Repair, rehabilitate, or replace?

A localized defect might be suitable for a point repair. A longer deteriorated reach may justify rehabilitation or replacement. Utilities also consider hydraulic capacity, access, nearby utilities, service interruptions, environmental consequences, and the risk of another failure. Emergency response protects people and the environment first; the permanent fix should address the underlying condition.

The takeaway

A sewer backup, wet spot, or overflow is a symptom. Understanding the pipe type and actual defect is what leads to a lasting solution.

Educational overview only. Field work requires utility-approved procedures, qualified personnel, and applicable safety requirements.
