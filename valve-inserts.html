Valve Inserts

Adding a permanent valve to an existing main while preserving service when conditions permit.

← Explore all valves & fittings

What is valve insertion?

Valve insertion puts a permanent shutoff valve into an existing water main where there was no valve before. With a suitable system, crews can install that valve while the main remains pressurized. That matters when a utility needs better control of its network but cannot easily shut down service to a neighborhood, business, or critical facility. The finished valve stays in place for future operation; it is not just equipment used during the repair.

The sleeve: the foundation of the installation

Every valve-insertion system uses a sleeve fitted around the existing pipe. The sleeve provides the connection to the installation equipment and forms part of the permanent assembly. Its design must match the pipe material, actual outside diameter, condition, and operating requirements. A pipe labeled “8-inch” does not tell you everything you need to know about its outside diameter or whether a particular sleeve will fit.

The temporary tapping valve and cutting equipment

In common live-installation arrangements, a temporary tapping gate valve is mounted above the sleeve. A tapping machine works through that open valve to make the required opening in the pipe. The cutting equipment is then withdrawn and the temporary valve closed, allowing equipment to be changed while the main remains contained. The exact opening and cutting method depend on the manufacturer and the valve-insertion design. This is why a valve insert is more than simply cutting a hole and dropping a valve into it.

The cartridge or bonnet: the valve that stays

After the opening is prepared, the system’s cartridge or bonnet assembly is installed using the equipment designed for that system. This assembly contains the working valve components that will ultimately open or close the main. Some manufacturers use a dedicated insertion machine; others adapt the tapping equipment with different tooling. The sleeve and cartridge or bonnet work together to create the permanent valve. The temporary tapping valve and installation machines are removed when the installation is completed according to the system design.

How it controls water

Think of a water main as a road with no intersection where traffic can be stopped. A valve insertion adds a permanent control point. When the new valve is open, water can pass through the main. When it is closed, it can isolate the intended section, subject to the way the surrounding distribution system is connected. That last point matters: a looped system may feed water from another direction, so one new valve does not automatically isolate every possible flow path.

Valve insertion versus line stopping

A line stop temporarily blocks flow so crews can complete another repair or modification. Once that work is finished, the stopping equipment is removed. A valve insertion creates a permanent operating valve that the utility can use years later. Both methods can help limit service interruptions, but they solve different problems. One is temporary isolation for a job; the other adds a lasting control point to the system.

What makes a good installation location?

Crews need to know more than the pipe size on the drawing. Pipe material, actual outside diameter, wall condition, pressure, nearby joints or fittings, available excavation space, and the surrounding network all affect the decision. The utility also needs to consider where the valve will be accessible for future operation. A perfectly installed valve in the wrong location may do little to reduce the next outage.

What happens after installation?

The completed valve and its seals must be checked using the applicable manufacturer and utility procedures. The valve’s location, size, direction of operation, and identification should be recorded so the next crew can find and use it. A valve nobody can locate is not nearly as useful as one that is documented and maintained.

Not the same as a line stop

A valve insert creates a permanent operating valve in the main, while a line stop provides temporary isolation for a particular job. Both require careful evaluation of pipe material, outside diameter, pressure, and condition. The installed valve must fit the utility’s future operation and maintenance needs.

Two common approaches to the final seal

All valve-insertion systems are designed for installation into a pressurized line. That live-installation capability is why they are used. Different manufacturers take different approaches to creating the finished valve seat and seal.

In one approach, the installation removes an opening from the upper part of the existing pipe and the closure seals against the remaining lower portion of that pipe. In another approach, the equipment removes a full section of pipe and the permanent valve seals within its own assembly. Neither approach should be assumed universal; the exact geometry and method are manufacturer-specific.

What both have in common is the finished product: a permanent, operable valve that provides a usable shutdown point for future maintenance or repairs. It is not a temporary line stop and is not removed when the immediate job ends.
