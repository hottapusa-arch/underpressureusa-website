Water Hammer and Pressure Surges

Rapid changes in water velocity can create pressure waves through a piping system.

← All Pumps & Pressure topics

What water hammer means

Water hammer is a rapid pressure change caused by a change in water velocity. A fast valve closure, sudden pump trip, or other quick change in flow can send a pressure wave through the system. The wave can reflect from boundaries and create alternating high and low pressures.

Why speed matters

The faster a flow change occurs relative to the system’s wave travel time, the more severe the resulting transient may be. Pipe material, diameter, wall thickness, restraint, system layout, and trapped air all influence the response. It is not accurate to treat every audible bang as the same type or severity of event.

Why the damage may appear somewhere else

A pressure transient travels through connected piping. The component that fails may be a weak joint, fitting, valve, or older pipe section some distance from the event that started the wave. Repeated transients can also add stress over time.

Air can make the problem more complicated

Air pockets change how the system responds during filling, draining, and flow changes. In some situations they cushion pressure changes; in others, moving or compressing air contributes to damaging events. Air-release and vacuum protection must be evaluated for the actual system, not chosen by a simple rule of thumb.

What utilities do about it

Possible controls include appropriate valve operating times, pump controls, surge tanks or vessels, air-management devices, and engineered transient analysis. The correct response depends on the cause. Slowing a valve may help in some situations, but it is not a substitute for diagnosing the system.

What to watch for

Repeated bangs, unexplained pressure spikes, frequent gasket failures, or problems after pump starts and stops deserve investigation. The goal is to understand the pressure event, not just replace the component that failed.
