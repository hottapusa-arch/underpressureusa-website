Water Pressure Basics

Pressure is the force water exerts against the inside of a pipe, valve, or fitting.

← All Pumps & Pressure topics

Pressure is the push; flow is the movement

Pressure describes force per unit area. Flow describes how much water passes a point over time. A gauge can show pressure even when no water is moving. Once a hydrant or tap opens, friction through pipes, fittings, and valves can lower the pressure available while water is flowing.

Elevation changes pressure

For water, a vertical elevation difference of about 2.31 feet corresponds to 1 psi, or about 0.433 psi per foot. In a connected, static system, a location 100 feet lower than another can have roughly 43 psi more pressure, assuming the same hydraulic grade and no other changes. This relationship is a useful starting point, not a complete prediction of operating pressure.

Static and residual pressure are different

Static pressure is measured with little or no local flow. Residual pressure is the pressure remaining during a flow test or other demand. A system with strong static pressure can still have poor performance under heavy demand if the supply, pipe size, or available pumping capacity is limited.

What pumps and tanks change

Pumps add energy to move water and maintain pressure under changing demand. Elevated storage provides hydraulic head and helps buffer demand and emergencies. Pressure-reducing valves may limit downstream pressure in lower areas. All three can affect what a field gauge shows.

What a gauge does not tell you

One pressure reading does not prove that a main has adequate flow capacity, that a fitting is properly rated, or that a surge will not occur. Time of day, pump operation, tank levels, and nearby demand can all change the reading. Always distinguish a measured value from an assumption about the whole system.

A simple field example

Two hydrants can be connected to the same distribution system and still show different pressures because one sits on higher ground. That does not automatically mean one hydrant is defective. Elevation and the way the network is operated may explain the difference.
