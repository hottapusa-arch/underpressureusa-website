Pressure Surges & Water Hammer

Why fast changes in flow can stress a system.

← All Infrastructure Failures topics

What a surge really is

When moving water changes velocity quickly, the resulting pressure disturbance travels through connected piping as a wave. A pump trip, rapid valve movement, or abrupt flow change can cause a transient. The wave may create both high-pressure and low-pressure conditions as it travels and reflects through the system.

Why a normal gauge may miss it

A standard pressure gauge may show acceptable operating pressure while missing a very brief transient. The severity depends on the rate of flow change, pipe characteristics, system geometry, and operating conditions. Fast-recording pressure equipment and an accurate timeline of pump and valve events can be important to an investigation.

Where damage can show up

The component that fails may be far from the pump or valve that initiated the event. Weak joints, fittings, restraints, and older pipe sections can be stressed by repeated cycles. Low-pressure portions of a transient may also create concerns about column separation or unwanted intrusion under certain conditions.

Air changes the picture

Trapped air and air movement can change transient behavior during filling, draining, and pump operation. Air-release and vacuum valves have specific functions, but they are not a universal cure for surge problems. Their location, sizing, and operating conditions must be evaluated for the actual system.

What crews can document

Record the sequence of operations, valve movement, pump starts or trips, noise, pressure readings, and locations of repeated failures. System-specific surge analysis and engineered controls may be needed; simply slowing every operation is not a substitute for understanding the cause.
