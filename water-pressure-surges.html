Pressure Surges & Water Hammer

Why fast changes in flow can stress a system.

← All Infrastructure Failures topics

What is happening?

Water is moving mass. When a pump stops or a valve closes quickly, the change can send a pressure wave through the pipe.

Why it happens

Surges can be positive or negative. Their size depends on how quickly flow changes, pipe characteristics, pump behavior, and the layout of the system. A pressure gauge showing normal conditions may miss a short event.

What crews look for

Repeated surges may stress joints, fittings, pipe walls, and equipment. A single major event can also create an immediate problem.

What we can learn

Utilities investigate operating sequences and pressure recordings, then evaluate surge-control measures appropriate to the system.

What matters in the field

Rapid changes in flow can create pressure waves that move through a pipeline. Both positive and negative transients can be important.

A practical example

A pressure surge can stress joints and fittings far from the pump or valve that triggered it.

The fastest change is often the hardest

A rapid change in flow can send pressure waves through a pipeline. Pump trips, valve closure, and moving air pockets may all contribute. The highest or lowest pressure can occur away from the event that triggered it, which is why system-wide behavior matters.
